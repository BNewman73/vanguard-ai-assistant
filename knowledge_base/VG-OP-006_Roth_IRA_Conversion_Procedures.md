## 1. What Is a Roth Conversion?

A Roth conversion is the movement of pre-tax retirement assets — from a Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or pre-tax 401(k) — into a Roth IRA. The amount converted is treated as ordinary income for the year of conversion. Once in the Roth IRA, future qualified withdrawals (principal and earnings) are tax-free.

**Key trade-off:** Pay taxes now to avoid taxes later. Conversions make the most sense when (a) your current marginal tax rate is lower than your expected future rate, or (b) you want to reduce future RMDs (see VG-OP-003), or (c) you're optimizing tax-bracket utilization.

## 2. Eligibility

### Who Can Convert
- Anyone with a Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA (after the 2-year holding period from first contribution), or pre-tax 401(k) (subject to plan rules and separation of service for in-service withdrawals)
- **There are no income limits on conversions** (unlike contribution limits to a Roth IRA)

### Who Should Hesitate
- Investors who would have to pay the conversion tax from the IRA itself (paying tax with IRA dollars dramatically reduces the math benefit)
- Investors near the start of Medicare premiums (IRMAA cliffs at certain MAGI levels)
- Investors who expect to be in a much lower tax bracket in retirement

## 3. The Pro-Rata Rule

If you have **any** pre-tax money in **any** Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, or SIMPLE IRA across all custodians, the IRS treats all of those balances as one pool. A conversion is then deemed to come proportionally from pre-tax and after-tax dollars.

**Example:** You have $90,000 pre-tax and $10,000 after-tax across all your Traditional IRAs. You convert $10,000 to a Roth.
- Pre-tax portion: $9,000 (90% of $10,000) — taxable
- After-tax portion: $1,000 (10% of $10,000) — not taxable

This rule defeats the "backdoor Roth" strategy for investors with substantial existing pre-tax IRA balances unless the pre-tax balance is rolled into a 401(k) first (effectively removing it from the IRA pro-rata pool).

The pro-rata rule does **not** apply to:
- 401(k) and 403(b) plan balances
- Roth IRA conversions made directly from a 401(k) (governed by separate plan rules)

## 4. The Backdoor Roth (For High-Earners)

High-earners (above the Roth IRA contribution income limits — $146,000 MAGI single / $230,000 MAGI MFJ for full contribution in 2026) can use the backdoor strategy:

1. Make a non-deductible contribution to a Traditional IRA (any income, $7,500 / $8,500 limit for 2026)
2. Convert the Traditional IRA balance to a Roth IRA
3. Pay tax only on any growth between contribution and conversion

**Effective only if:** You have no other pre-tax IRA balances (because of pro-rata rule). If you do, consolidate those into a 401(k) first if your plan accepts rollovers.

## 7. The 5-Year Rules

There are **two separate 5-year rules** for Roth IRAs. Both must be understood:

### 7.1 The Owner's Roth IRA 5-Year Rule

Earnings can be withdrawn tax-free only if:
1. The Roth IRA has been open for at least 5 years (clock starts January 1 of the tax year of your first Roth IRA contribution or conversion to **any** Roth IRA), AND
2. The owner is age 59-1/2 or older (or meets another qualifying exception, e.g., first-time home purchase up to $10,000 lifetime)

### 7.2 The Conversion 5-Year Rule

Each conversion has its own 5-year clock. If you withdraw converted principal before age 59-1/2 and within 5 years of that specific conversion, you owe a 10% early-withdrawal penalty on the converted amount (but not income tax, since you already paid that at conversion).

After age 59-1/2, the conversion 5-year rule no longer applies.

## 8. Recharacterization (No Longer Available)

Prior to 2018, Roth conversions could be "undone" via recharacterization. **The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act eliminated recharacterization of conversions** effective for conversions made in 2018 or later. Conversions are now permanent. This makes pre-conversion planning more important.

## 9. Common Strategies

### 9.1 Bracket-Filling
Convert just enough each year to fill up your current marginal tax bracket without spilling into a higher bracket.

### 9.2 Conversion Ladder (Early Retirement)
Convert in low-income years (e.g., first years of early retirement before Social Security starts), creating a stream of converted principal that is penalty-free to withdraw 5 years later.

### 9.3 Down-Market Conversions
Converting after a market drop reduces the dollar amount taxed at conversion. Future recoveries grow tax-free in the Roth.

### 9.4 Mega Backdoor Roth (via 401(k))
For plans that allow after-tax 401(k) contributions plus in-plan Roth conversions or in-service rollouts, the strategy can shelter much larger amounts than the IRA backdoor.

## 10. Risks and Pitfalls

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Pushed into higher tax bracket by conversion | Pre-calculate; consider partial conversions over multiple years |
| IRMAA (Medicare premium surcharge) trigger | Watch MAGI cliffs at $103K/$206K and higher tiers |
| State tax surprise | Some states don't conform to federal Roth treatment |
| Pro-rata rule unexpectedly applied | Consolidate pre-tax IRA dollars into a 401(k) first |
| 5-year-rule miscalculation on later withdrawal | Track conversion-year clocks separately |
| Paying conversion tax with IRA dollars | Always pay tax with non-IRA funds if possible |

## 11. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I convert a portion of my IRA?**
Yes. Partial conversions are allowed and often more tax-efficient than full conversions.

**Q: Will the conversion affect my RMD this year?**
RMDs for the year of conversion must be taken **before** the conversion (RMD amounts cannot be converted). After conversion, the converted balance is no longer subject to RMDs (because Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMD requirement during the original owner's lifetime — see VG-OP-003).

**Q: How long does the conversion take to settle?**
Same business day if requested before 4:00 PM ET online. The taxable event occurs on the conversion date.

**Q: Can I undo it?**
No. Recharacterization was eliminated in 2018.

**Q: Does the conversion count as a contribution?**
No. Conversions and contributions are separate categories. You can still contribute up to the annual limit ($7,500/$8,500 for 2026) for the year on top of any conversion.
